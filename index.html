The Hardest Thing Yet, Something People Do for Love

By Sendongkaba Jamir, 10 minute read

There are moments when certain thoughts keep returning to our minds, running through our heads every hour until we can no longer ignore them. Sometimes, these thoughts feel like messages from a higher power, a whisper from the universe urging us to pause, reflect, and see life from a different perspective. For me, one such thought has become impossible to ignore: some of the hardest things people do in life are not for fame, recognition, or success, but simply for love.

Hey, I am Sendongkaba Jamir, a young man who turned 22 in July 2026. As long as I have air in my lungs, I try to find reasons to laugh and smile, even in the smallest things. Yet, 2026 has turned out to be one of the most fascinating and life-changing years of my life. It has not always been filled with flowers and sunshine. Like every meaningful journey, it has brought its share of ups and downs, challenges, and lessons that have changed the way I look at life.

Four months ago, I graduated. Until then, I had largely depended on my parents for my needs, as I had done throughout my life. But after graduation, I decided it was time to step outside my comfort zone and experience life beyond the classroom. I wanted to understand the world not merely through books and theories but through real experiences.

And so, I began working.

I worked on a farm, earning ₹600 a day. I also worked in construction, loading and unloading bricks for ₹300 per loading and unloading task. These were some of the jobs I took up during those four months, and I am proud of every one of them. There is dignity in earning through honest work, regardless of how much one earns or what society thinks of the job.

Those experiences taught me lessons that no classroom could have taught me in quite the same way.

The Value of Money and the Dignity of Labour

Before experiencing these jobs firsthand, I had never fully understood the value of money. I knew that money was important, of course, but knowing something intellectually is very different from experiencing it personally.

When you work under the scorching sun, feel your body grow weaker with every passing hour, and realise that your daily earnings depend on how much physical work you can endure, money begins to mean something different. Every rupee carries the weight of your sweat, your exhaustion, and your time.

More importantly, my respect for labourers grew immensely. I began to look at their lives differently and even found myself comparing their struggles with those of people preparing for competitive examinations or working in professional careers.

In India, examinations such as the Union Public Service Commission’s Civil Services Examination are widely regarded as highly demanding. Preparing for them requires discipline, patience, consistency, and tremendous mental strength. Those who succeed deserve recognition for their dedication and perseverance.

However, while society often celebrates such achievements, we sometimes overlook the people who perform physically demanding work every single day just to provide for their families.

I began to wonder: Why do we so readily recognise the hardships of some professions while overlooking the struggles of others?

Is mental exhaustion more deserving of respect than physical exhaustion? Is a person sitting in an air-conditioned office necessarily working harder than someone labouring under the burning sun?

I do not believe that every profession can be compared so easily. Each has its own challenges. But I do believe that the dignity of a person should never be measured by the title of their job, the amount they earn, or the social status attached to their profession.

Every honest worker deserves respect.

Two Days That Changed My Perspective

I experienced the harshness of agricultural labour firsthand when I worked in the fields under the scorching sun.

I continued for two days, but by the end of that time, my mind and body could no longer bear the strain. The physical exhaustion was overwhelming, and I could not continue from the third day.

I had to stop.

That experience humbled me.

I had managed only two days before my body gave up, yet countless people perform similar work day after day, year after year. Some continue for decades, enduring the heat, the exhaustion, and the physical pain that come with their livelihoods.

They wake up each morning knowing that another demanding day awaits them. They work not necessarily because they enjoy the hardship but because their responsibilities leave them with little choice.

And that was when a question began to trouble my mind.

What gives these people the strength to keep going when their bodies are exhausted and their work offers so little comfort?

The answer, I believe, often lies in love.

Love for their children. Love for their families. Love for the people who depend on them.

Many labourers continue working despite their age, their pain, and their exhaustion because they want their children to have opportunities they never had. They want to see their sons and daughters graduate, pursue higher education, secure stable jobs, and build better lives.

Some parents spend their entire lives working with their hands so that their children may one day work with their minds.

They sacrifice their comfort so their children can experience a little more of it. They endure hardship so their children might have a chance to live with fewer hardships of their own.

And sometimes, the children who benefit from these sacrifices may never fully understand the price their parents paid.

Perhaps that is one of the most heartbreaking and beautiful realities of life: parents often carry burdens their children may never see, all in the hope that those children will one day have lighter burdens to carry.

The Struggle We Often Fail to See

People often say that one of the hardest things a person can do is crack the UPSC Civil Services Examination. I agree that it is an extraordinary challenge. It demands years of preparation, immense dedication, and the ability to overcome repeated disappointments.

But I also believe that the person who works as a daily labourer deserves equal dignity and respect.

Imagine spending eight to ten hours studying in a room, sitting in the shade, or working in an office. These activities can be mentally exhausting, and I do not intend to diminish their difficulty. Every demanding pursuit requires effort, and mental labour can be just as challenging in its own way.

But imagine spending those same hours carrying bricks, working on a farm under the scorching sun, or performing repetitive physical tasks while your body grows increasingly tired.

The difference becomes clear when you experience it yourself.

I would encourage anyone who has the opportunity to understand the lives of daily wage workers to spend time learning about their work and their struggles. Even a brief experience can change the way you perceive labour, money, privilege, and the people who work tirelessly around you.

I learned this lesson not because I worked for years in the fields or on construction sites, but because I experienced a small part of that hardship myself.

Two days were enough to make me realise how little I understood before.

If my body struggled to endure even a few days, what about those who have no choice but to continue for months and years? What about those who work despite illness, age, exhaustion, and the uncertainty of whether tomorrow will bring enough income to feed their families?

What about the parents who return home after a long day of labour, hide their exhaustion, and smile at their children because they do not want them to worry?

We may see their tired faces, rough hands, and worn-out clothes, but we may never see the dreams they carry or the sacrifices they make in silence.

Their work may not always bring them recognition, but its impact can shape generations.

Love Wears Many Faces

When we talk about love, we often imagine romantic relationships, beautiful words, thoughtful gifts, and memorable moments. We associate love with the things people say and the affection they openly express.

But love does not always look beautiful.

Sometimes, love looks like a father leaving home before sunrise to work at a construction site.

Sometimes, it looks like a mother working in a field until her back aches, only to return home and continue caring for her family.

Sometimes, it looks like rough hands counting a few hard-earned rupees, carefully deciding how much to spend and how much to save for a child’s education.

Sometimes, love is a tired person waking up the next morning and returning to the same difficult work because someone at home is counting on them.

There may be no applause, no certificates, no public recognition, and no guarantee of success. There may only be exhaustion, responsibility, and the hope that all the sacrifices will eventually mean something.

Yet they continue.

They continue because their dreams are no longer just their own. They are working for the people they love, for the futures they imagine, and for the opportunities they want their children to have.

Perhaps this is why some of the greatest expressions of love go unnoticed. They are not always spoken aloud. They are demonstrated through years of sacrifice, quiet perseverance, and the willingness to endure discomfort for someone else’s happiness.

Not every act of love comes with a smile. Some come with sweat, pain, exhaustion, and hands that never stop working.

A Newfound Respect

These past four months have taught me more than I expected. They have helped me understand the value of money, the dignity of labour, and the struggles that many people face simply to keep their families going.

Most importantly, they have taught me humility.

I have realised that we should be careful about judging people based on their professions. A person’s occupation tells us what they do to earn a living, but it does not tell us the depth of their sacrifices, the strength of their character, or the love they carry for their families.

A person working in an office deserves respect. A student preparing for a competitive examination deserves respect. And a labourer carrying bricks under the scorching sun deserves respect, too.

Respect should not be reserved only for those who hold prestigious positions or achieve socially celebrated milestones. It should also extend to those whose work is physically demanding, whose earnings are modest, and whose contributions are essential to the lives we often take for granted.

I am proud that I experienced these jobs, even if I could not continue doing them for long. They gave me a glimpse into a reality that I might otherwise have continued to overlook.

I may not know everything about the lives of labourers, and my brief experience cannot compare with the years of hardship many of them endure. But it has given me a reason to listen more carefully, judge less quickly, and appreciate more deeply.

Sometimes, all it takes is stepping outside our comfort zones to discover how much we still have to learn.

The Hardest Thing Yet, Something People Do for Love

As I reflect on these experiences, one thought continues to return to my mind.

What is the hardest thing a person can do in life?

Is it clearing one of the most competitive examinations? Is it working towards a dream despite repeated failures? Is it overcoming the challenges that stand between us and success?

Perhaps all of these are difficult in their own ways.

But there is another kind of struggle that deserves to be acknowledged: the struggle of a person who wakes up every day, puts their body through exhausting work, and continues despite the pain because someone they love depends on them.

They may never receive a prestigious title. They may never be celebrated by society. They may never have the opportunity to tell their stories to the world.

But they keep going.

Not because the work is easy, nor because the hardship is pleasant, but because love gives their sacrifices a purpose.

My experience as a farm worker and a construction labourer lasted only a short time, but the lesson it left with me will remain for much longer. I now understand that behind many ordinary lives are extraordinary sacrifices, and behind many ordinary paychecks are hours of struggle that money alone can never fully measure.

To all the labourers working in fields, construction sites, factories, and countless other places, I offer my deepest respect. Your work matters. Your struggles matter. Your lives matter.

You deserve dignity not because of what you provide for others, but because you are human beings whose efforts help keep families, communities, and society moving forward.

And to the children whose parents labour tirelessly for their future, I hope you never forget the sacrifices that may have helped you reach where you are today.

We may celebrate those who conquer difficult examinations, achieve professional success, and fulfil their ambitions. We should continue to do so. But let us also remember those who spend their lives working towards a dream they may never personally enjoy: seeing their children live a better life.

Because sometimes, the hardest thing a person can do is not to achieve something extraordinary for themselves, but to endure something extraordinary for someone else.

It was hard. It is hard. And it will continue to be hard. Yet, people do it every day, not always for themselves, but for love.

Written by Sendongkaba Jamir.
